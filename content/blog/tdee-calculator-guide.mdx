Your TDEE is the single most important number in nutrition. It tells you exactly how many calories your body burns in a day — and every weight loss, muscle gain, or maintenance plan starts here.

The problem is that most people either don't know their TDEE, use an inaccurate calculator that overestimates their activity level, or confuse TDEE with BMR and end up eating far too little. All three mistakes lead to the same outcome: frustration, stalled progress, and eventually giving up.

This guide covers what TDEE actually is, how to calculate yours accurately, and — most importantly — how to use that number to get real results. <Callout type="info">
**Quick answer:** TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) is the total number of calories you burn per day, including your resting metabolism, physical activity, and food digestion. Most adults have a TDEE between 1,600 and 3,000 calories per day. Calculate yours by finding your BMR using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, then multiplying by an activity factor (1.2 for sedentary to 1.9 for extremely active).

## What Is TDEE (and Why Should You Care)?

Your TDEE is made up of four components:

**Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)** accounts for roughly 60–70% of your total daily burn. This is the energy your body needs just to stay alive — keeping your heart beating, your lungs breathing, your brain functioning, and your cells regenerating. Even if you lay in bed all day and did absolutely nothing, your body would still burn this many calories. BMR is primarily determined by your body size, muscle mass, age, and sex.

**Thermic Effect of Food (TEF)** accounts for approximately 10% of your TDEE. This is the energy your body uses to digest, absorb, and metabolize the food you eat. Not all foods cost the same energy to process — [research published in *Nutrition & Metabolism*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15507129/) shows that protein has a thermic effect of 20–30%, meaning your body burns 20–30% of protein calories just digesting them, compared to 5–10% for carbohydrates and 0–3% for fat.

**Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (NEAT)** accounts for 15–30% of your TDEE and is the most variable component. NEAT includes every movement you make that isn't structured exercise — walking to the car, fidgeting at your desk, standing while cooking, carrying groceries, even gesturing while talking. [A landmark study published in *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9880251/) found that NEAT can vary by up to 2,000 calories per day between individuals, making it the single biggest differentiator in total daily calorie burn between people of similar size.

**Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (EAT)** typically accounts for only 5–10% of total TDEE for most people — far less than most assume. A 45-minute gym session might burn 200–400 calories, but that's a small fraction of the 2,000+ calories your body burns through the other three components combined.

Understanding these components reveals an important truth: **your workout is not the main driver of your calorie burn.** Your resting metabolism and daily movement (NEAT) are. This is why you can't out-exercise a bad diet, and why knowing your full TDEE — not just your exercise calories — is essential for weight management.

## How to Calculate Your TDEE (Step by Step)

### Step 1 — Find Your BMR

Your Basal Metabolic Rate is the starting point. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation calculates it based on your weight, height, age, and sex:

**For men:**
BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) + 5

**For women:**
BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age in years) − 161

**Worked example:** A 32-year-old woman who weighs 68 kg (150 lbs) and stands 165 cm (5'5") tall:

BMR = (10 × 68) + (6.25 × 165) − (5 × 32) − 161
BMR = 680 + 1,031 − 160 − 161
**BMR = 1,390 calories per day**

This means her body burns approximately 1,390 calories just to maintain basic life functions at complete rest. This is not her calorie target — it's only the foundation for the next step.

### Step 2 — Multiply by Your Activity Factor

Your BMR only accounts for resting energy. To find your actual TDEE, multiply it by an activity multiplier that reflects how much you move throughout the day — including both structured exercise and general daily activity.

| Activity Level | Description | Multiplier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sedentary** | Desk job, little to no exercise | 1.2 |
| **Lightly active** | Light exercise 1–3 days per week | 1.375 |
| **Moderately active** | Moderate exercise 3–5 days per week | 1.55 |
| **Very active** | Hard exercise 6–7 days per week | 1.725 |
| **Extremely active** | Athlete, physical job + daily training | 1.9 |

**Continuing the example:** If our 32-year-old woman exercises moderately (gym 3–4 times per week):

TDEE = 1,390 × 1.55
**TDEE = 2,155 calories per day**

This is her maintenance level — the amount she'd eat to stay at her current weight.

**A critical note on activity levels:** Most people overestimate where they fall. A [study in the *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23174226/) found that people consistently overreport their physical activity levels by 36–173%. If you work a desk job and go to the gym 3 times per week, you're likely "lightly active" (1.375), not "moderately active" (1.55). When in doubt, choose the lower activity level — you can always adjust upward later based on real-world results.

### Step 3 — Adjust for Your Goal

Your TDEE is your maintenance number. From here, adjust based on what you're trying to achieve:

**To lose weight:** Subtract 300–500 calories from your TDEE. This creates a moderate deficit that produces approximately 0.5–1 pound of fat loss per week — a rate that [research in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002828/) associates with better long-term maintenance and less muscle loss.

**To maintain weight:** Eat at your TDEE.

**To build muscle:** Add 250–500 calories above your TDEE, combined with resistance training and adequate protein.

**For our example:**

- Weight loss target: 2,155 − 500 = **1,655 calories per day**
- Maintenance: **2,155 calories per day**
- Muscle gain: 2,155 + 300 = **2,455 calories per day**

## TDEE vs. BMR — Why the Difference Matters

This is one of the most common and most damaging mix-ups in nutrition.

**BMR** = calories burned at complete rest (lying in bed, doing nothing).
**TDEE** = BMR + all daily movement + exercise + food digestion.

For most people, TDEE is 40–90% higher than BMR. The 32-year-old woman in our example has a BMR of 1,390 but a TDEE of 2,155 — a difference of 765 calories.

Why does this matter? Because if you mistake your BMR for your TDEE and eat at your BMR level (1,390 calories) while living a moderately active life, you've accidentally created a 765-calorie daily deficit. That's extremely aggressive — the kind of deficit that triggers metabolic adaptation, significant muscle loss, intense hunger, and eventual binge-eating.

[Research in the *International Journal of Obesity*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11360143/) consistently shows that very aggressive deficits (more than 500–750 calories below TDEE) lead to greater muscle loss, faster metabolic slowdown, and higher rates of weight regain compared to moderate deficits.

The bottom line: **never eat at your BMR unless directed by a medical professional.** Your calorie target should be based on your TDEE minus a moderate deficit — not your BMR.

## Why Most TDEE Calculators Give You the Wrong Number

Every TDEE calculator — including ours — gives you an *estimate*, not a measurement. The activity multipliers are population averages that may not perfectly match your individual physiology. Here's where inaccuracies typically come from:

**Activity level selection is subjective.** The difference between "lightly active" and "moderately active" is 200–400 calories per day for most people. Choosing the wrong one throws off your entire calculation.

**Muscle mass isn't accounted for.** Two people at the same height, weight, age, and activity level can have very different TDEEs if one has significantly more muscle mass. Muscle is metabolically expensive — [research in the *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11319656/) estimates that each kilogram of muscle burns approximately 13 calories per day at rest, compared to about 4.5 calories per kilogram of fat.

**NEAT varies day to day.** Your non-exercise movement changes based on your mood, energy, workload, and sleep quality. On a busy day when you're walking between meetings, your NEAT might be 400 calories. On a day you're glued to your desk, it might be 100.

## How to Use Your TDEE for Weight Loss

Once you have your TDEE, weight loss becomes a straightforward (though not easy) math problem:

**Eat 300–500 calories below your TDEE consistently.** At a 500-calorie daily deficit, you'll lose approximately 1 pound per week. At 300, about 0.6 pounds per week. Both are effective and sustainable.

**Don't go below 1,200 calories (women) or 1,500 calories (men) without medical supervision.** Below these thresholds, you risk nutrient deficiencies, muscle loss, hormonal disruption, and the metabolic adaptation that makes future weight loss harder. The [National Institutes of Health](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/adult-overweight-obesity/treatment) recommends these as minimum intake floors for safe dieting.

**Recalculate every 10–15 pounds lost.** As you lose weight, your body requires fewer calories — your BMR drops because there's less mass to maintain, and your NEAT often decreases unconsciously. A TDEE that worked at 180 pounds will be too high at 165 pounds. Recalculate using the [TDEE calculator](/tdee-calculator) and adjust your target.

**Focus on protein.** Within your calorie target, aim for 0.7–1 gram of protein per pound of body weight. [A meta-analysis published in *The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26883880/) found that higher protein intake during calorie restriction significantly preserves lean muscle mass and improves body composition compared to standard protein diets — even at the same calorie deficit. ## How to Use Your TDEE for Muscle Gain

Building muscle requires a caloric surplus — you need to give your body extra energy to repair and grow new muscle tissue after resistance training.

**Eat 250–500 calories above your TDEE.** A smaller surplus (250–300) minimizes fat gain while still supporting muscle growth, sometimes called a "lean bulk." A larger surplus (400–500) accelerates muscle gain but comes with more fat accumulation.

**Protein becomes even more important.** During a surplus, aim for 0.8–1 gram of protein per pound of body weight. [A systematic review in *Sports Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) found that protein intakes above 1.6 grams per kilogram per day showed diminishing returns for muscle growth in most trained individuals. For a 170-pound person, that's roughly 120–170 grams of protein per day.

**You need a resistance training stimulus.** A caloric surplus without strength training just creates fat gain. The extra calories only build muscle if you're giving your muscles a reason to grow. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good TDEE for weight loss?

There's no universal "good" TDEE — it depends entirely on your body. But your *weight loss calorie target* should be 300–500 calories below your personal TDEE. For most women, this lands between 1,400–1,800 calories per day. For most men, 1,800–2,400. Use the [TDEE calculator](/tdee-calculator) to find your specific number.

### How accurate are TDEE calculators?

TDEE calculators are estimates, not measurements. They're typically accurate within 10–15% for most people, which is a good starting point. The most common source of error is selecting the wrong activity level. To get a more precise number, use the calculator as a baseline and calibrate it against your actual weight trend over 2–3 weeks of consistent tracking.

### Should I eat my TDEE to lose weight?

No — eating at your TDEE maintains your current weight. To lose weight, eat *below* your TDEE. A deficit of 300–500 calories per day is the recommended range for sustainable fat loss. Eating at your TDEE is appropriate if your goal is weight maintenance.

### What's the difference between TDEE and maintenance calories?

They're the same thing. TDEE *is* your maintenance calorie level — the number of calories you need to consume to stay at your current weight. The terms are used interchangeably.

### How often should I recalculate my TDEE?

Every 10–15 pounds of weight change, or every 6–8 weeks during active dieting. As your body weight changes, your TDEE changes with it. A person who has lost 20 pounds has a meaningfully lower TDEE than when they started — continuing to eat at their original target will eventually stall progress.

### Is 1,500 calories below my TDEE safe?

A 1,500-calorie deficit is extremely aggressive and not recommended for most people. Deficits larger than 750–1,000 calories per day significantly increase the risk of muscle loss, metabolic slowdown, nutrient deficiencies, and eventual weight regain. Stick to a 300–500 calorie deficit for sustainable results. If you feel you need a larger deficit, consult a healthcare professional first.

### Why am I not losing weight even though I'm eating below my TDEE?

The most common reason is that you're not actually eating below your TDEE — tracking errors (forgotten snacks, underestimated portions, uncounted cooking oils) close the gap between your intended deficit and your real intake. Other factors include metabolic adaptation from prolonged dieting, water retention masking fat loss, and overestimating your activity level when calculating TDEE.
