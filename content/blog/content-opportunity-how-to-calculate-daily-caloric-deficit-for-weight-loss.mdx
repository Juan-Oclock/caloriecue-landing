# How to Calculate Daily Caloric Deficit for Weight Loss

Understanding your daily caloric deficit is the single most important skill you can develop on your weight loss journey. It removes the guesswork and replaces it with a clear, math-backed number that works with your body — not against it.

No crash diets. No extreme restriction. Just a consistent daily target that moves the needle.

This guide walks you through the exact steps, from finding your maintenance calories all the way to hitting your daily target with confidence.

---

## What Is a Caloric Deficit?

A caloric deficit happens when you eat fewer calories than your body burns in a day. When that gap is consistent, your body turns to stored fat for energy — and that's when the scale starts to move.

The math is straightforward: one pound of body fat contains roughly 3,500 calories. A daily deficit of 500 calories leads to approximately one pound of fat loss per week. That principle underlies almost every sustainable weight loss plan.

---

## Step 1: Find Your TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure)

Before you can create a deficit, you need your baseline — the number of calories your body burns on an average day. This is your **TDEE**, or Total Daily Energy Expenditure.

Your TDEE accounts for:

- **BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate)** — calories burned just to keep you alive (breathing, heart beating, digestion)
- **Activity level** — calories burned through movement, exercise, and daily tasks

The most reliable formula is the **Mifflin-St Jeor Equation**, validated in research and widely used by registered dietitians. According to a review published by the [National Institutes of Health](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2719843/), it is the most accurate predictive equation for resting metabolic rate in both healthy and overweight individuals:

**For men:**

BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5

**For women:**

BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Once you have your BMR, multiply by an activity factor:

| Activity Level | Multiplier |

|---|---|

| Sedentary (desk job, little movement) | × 1.2 |

| Lightly active (light exercise 1–3 days/week) | × 1.375 |

| Moderately active (moderate exercise 3–5 days/week) | × 1.55 |

| Very active (hard exercise 6–7 days/week) | × 1.725 |

| Extra active (physical job + daily training) | × 1.9 |

The result is your TDEE — your maintenance calorie number. Eat consistently at this level and your weight stays stable.

## Step 2: Choose Your Deficit Size

This is where most people go wrong. They slash calories dramatically, lose weight fast for two weeks, then stall — or rebound completely.

Here's a practical framework for choosing a deficit that actually lasts:

### Moderate Deficit: 250–500 Calories/Day

This is the sweet spot for most people. A 250-calorie daily deficit targets roughly 0.5 lb of loss per week. A 500-calorie deficit targets about 1 lb per week.

You'll preserve more muscle, feel less hungry, and be far more likely to stick with it long term. A meta-analysis in [Obesity Reviews](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-789X.2006.00241.x) consistently shows that moderate, sustained deficits outperform aggressive ones for long-term fat loss.

### Aggressive Deficit: 500–750 Calories/Day

Appropriate for people with more weight to lose or those who want faster initial results. Still manageable for many, especially when protein intake is high and food choices are satisfying.

Going beyond 750–1,000 calories below maintenance is not recommended without medical supervision. Large deficits increase the risk of muscle loss, nutrient deficiencies, and metabolic adaptation.

### A Good Starting Point

For most people, a **500-calorie daily deficit** is the standard starting recommendation. It's sustainable, produces visible results, and doesn't require you to live on salad and willpower.

## Step 3: Calculate Your Daily Calorie Target

Now that you know how to calculate daily caloric deficit for weight loss, the formula is simple:

**Daily calorie target = TDEE − deficit**

Here's a worked example:

- Woman, 32 years old, 68 kg, 165 cm, moderately active
- BMR = (10 × 68) + (6.25 × 165) − (5 × 32) − 161 = **1,470 calories**
- TDEE = 1,470 × 1.55 = **2,279 calories/day**
- Target with 500-cal deficit = **1,779 calories/day**

Eating around 1,780 calories per day puts her in a consistent caloric deficit — roughly 1 lb of loss per week without feeling deprived.

## Step 4: Track Your Intake Against Your Target

Knowing your target is only half the equation. You have to track what you actually eat.

Here are three practical approaches:

### Option 1: Log Every Meal (Most Accurate)

Use a calorie tracking app to log everything. Weigh portions when possible, especially for calorie-dense foods like oils, nuts, and grains. This takes about 5–10 minutes per day once you're used to it.

Apps like CalorieCue make this easier with AI-powered photo logging — snap a picture of your meal and get an instant calorie estimate. No barcode scanning, no manual entry for every ingredient.

### Option 2: Use a Weekly Average

Instead of hitting your exact number every day, aim for your weekly total. If your target is 1,800 calories/day, your weekly budget is 12,600 calories. Some days you'll eat more, others less — and that's completely fine.

This approach reduces stress and still produces consistent results over time.

### Option 3: Portion-Based Tracking

If logging numbers feels overwhelming, start with visual portion control. It won't be as precise, but it builds calorie awareness while you ease into the habit. Add more precision later as tracking becomes second nature.

## How to Adjust When Results Stall

Your TDEE isn't a fixed number. As you lose weight, your body burns fewer calories at rest — so the same deficit that worked at the start will eventually stop producing results.

Every 4–6 weeks, reassess:

- Lost weight consistently? Keep going.
- Stalled for 2+ weeks despite accurate tracking? Recalculate your TDEE at your new weight, or reduce intake by another 100–150 calories.
- Losing too fast (more than 1.5 lbs/week consistently)? Add 100–200 calories back to protect muscle.

This recalibration is normal and expected. It's not failure — it's your body adapting, which means the deficit is working.

## Common Mistakes When Calculating Your Deficit

Even with the right formula, these easy errors can undermine your results:

**Overestimating activity level.** Most people choose "moderately active" when they're actually sedentary. When in doubt, go one level lower and adjust upward only if you're not losing.

**Missing liquid calories.** Coffee drinks, juices, sports drinks, and alcohol add up fast and are easy to overlook.

**Forgetting to recalculate.** Your TDEE changes as your weight drops. Recalculate every 5–10 lbs lost to keep your daily caloric deficit accurate.

**Treating your target as a ceiling.** The goal isn't to eat as little as possible — it's to hit your target consistently. Eating far below your target can slow your metabolism and trigger rebound eating.

## The Bottom Line

Mastering this calculation is one of the most empowering steps you can take for your health. It replaces guesswork with clarity, and restriction with intention.

Here's the recap:

1. Calculate your TDEE using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation and your activity multiplier
2. Subtract 250–500 calories to set your daily deficit target
3. Track your intake consistently — use whatever method works for you
4. Recalculate every 4–6 weeks as your weight changes

You don't need to be perfect. You just need to be consistent.
