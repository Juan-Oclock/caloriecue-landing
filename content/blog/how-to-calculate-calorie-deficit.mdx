You've heard that a calorie deficit is the key to weight loss — but how do you actually calculate one that works for *your* body?

Here's the truth: a calorie deficit simply means consuming fewer calories than your body burns. That's it. No special foods, no magic timing, no supplement required. But the difference between a deficit that leads to sustainable weight loss and one that leaves you hungry, tired, and right back where you started comes down to how you set it up.

In this guide, we'll walk through the exact steps to calculate your calorie deficit — from finding your baseline metabolism to setting a realistic target — plus the common mistakes that stall progress and how to avoid them.

## What Is a Calorie Deficit?

A calorie deficit occurs when you consume fewer calories than your body needs to maintain its current weight. Think of it like a bank account: your body has a daily energy budget (called **maintenance calories**). When you consistently "spend" more energy than you "deposit" through food, your body taps into stored energy — primarily body fat — to make up the difference.

This concept is known as **energy balance**:

- **Caloric surplus** (eating more than you burn) → weight gain
- **Caloric maintenance** (eating roughly what you burn) → weight stays the same
- **Caloric deficit** (eating less than you burn) → weight loss

<Callout type="info">
**The core definition:** A calorie deficit is the gap between the calories you consume and the calories your body expends. A deficit of roughly 3,500 calories results in approximately one pound of fat loss — though individual results vary based on metabolism, body composition, and activity level.
</Callout>

The size of your deficit determines how quickly you lose weight — but also how sustainable the process feels. A moderate deficit lets you lose fat while preserving muscle, maintaining energy, and actually enjoying what you eat.

## Why a Calorie Deficit Is the Only Way to Lose Fat

This might sound like an oversimplification, but it's backed by decades of metabolic research: **every successful fat loss approach works because it creates a calorie deficit**. The laws of thermodynamics apply to your body just as they do everywhere else.

That means:

- **Keto works** not because carbs are inherently fattening, but because cutting an entire macronutrient group tends to reduce total calorie intake
- **Intermittent fasting works** not because of a special "fasting window," but because eating in a shorter window often means eating less overall
- **Low-fat diets work** for the same reason — fewer calories from fat means fewer total calories

None of these approaches are magic. They're all different strategies to achieve the same outcome: energy balance tilted toward a deficit.

A research review published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* compared dozens of named diets and concluded that **total calorie intake — not diet composition — was the primary driver of weight loss**. The best diet is the one that helps you maintain a consistent, moderate deficit without making you miserable.

## Step 1: Calculate Your BMR

Your **Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)** is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest — just to keep you alive. This covers breathing, circulating blood, regulating temperature, and maintaining organ function. For most people, BMR accounts for 60-70% of total daily calorie burn.

The most widely used and research-validated formula is the **Mifflin-St Jeor equation**:

- **Men:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age) + 5
- **Women:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) - (5 × age) - 161

**Example calculation:**
A 30-year-old woman who weighs 70 kg (154 lbs) and is 165 cm (5'5") tall:

BMR = (10 × 70) + (6.25 × 165) - (5 × 30) - 161
BMR = 700 + 1,031 - 150 - 161
**BMR = 1,420 calories/day**

This means her body burns approximately 1,420 calories per day just by existing — before any movement or activity.

## Step 2: Find Your TDEE

Your BMR only tells you what you burn at rest. To find out how many calories you actually burn in a day, you need your **[Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)](/tdee-calculator)**. This accounts for all the movement and activity in your life — from walking to the kitchen to a full workout.

To estimate your TDEE, multiply your BMR by an **activity factor**:

| Activity Level | Description | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| **Sedentary** | Desk job, little to no exercise | 1.2 |
| **Lightly active** | Light exercise 1-3 days/week | 1.375 |
| **Moderately active** | Moderate exercise 3-5 days/week | 1.55 |
| **Very active** | Hard exercise 6-7 days/week | 1.725 |
| **Extremely active** | Athlete or very physical job + exercise | 1.9 |

**Continuing our example:**
Our 30-year-old woman exercises moderately (3-4 days per week):

TDEE = 1,420 × 1.55
**TDEE = 2,201 calories/day**

This is her estimated maintenance calories — the amount she'd need to eat to stay at her current weight. To lose weight, she'll need to eat below this number.

<Callout type="info">
**How many calories should you eat?** Your TDEE is the starting point. Most people's TDEE falls between 1,800 and 2,800 calories depending on size, age, sex, and activity level. Knowing yours gives you a concrete number to work from — rather than guessing or following a generic 2,000-calorie recommendation.
</Callout>

## Step 3: Set Your Calorie Deficit

Now for the part everyone wants to know: **how much should you subtract?**

The classic recommendation is a **500-calorie deficit per day**, which translates to roughly one pound of fat loss per week (since one pound of body fat contains approximately 3,500 calories). This is often called the "500-calorie rule."

Here's how different deficit sizes compare:

| Daily Deficit | Weekly Fat Loss | Sustainability |
|---|---|---|
| **250 calories** | ~0.5 lb/week | Very sustainable, ideal for those close to goal weight |
| **500 calories** | ~1 lb/week | The sweet spot for most people |
| **750 calories** | ~1.5 lbs/week | Aggressive — suitable for those with significant weight to lose |
| **1,000 calories** | ~2 lbs/week | Very aggressive — hard to sustain, higher risk of muscle loss |

**For our example:**
With a TDEE of 2,201 calories, a 500-calorie deficit means eating approximately **1,700 calories per day** to lose about one pound per week.

The recommended range for most people is a **300-500 calorie deficit**. This is large enough to produce visible results within a few weeks but small enough to avoid the hunger, fatigue, and muscle loss that come with extreme dieting.

<Callout type="warning">
**Avoid extreme deficits.** Eating below 1,200 calories per day (for women) or 1,500 calories (for men) is generally not recommended without medical supervision. Very low calorie intake can slow your metabolism, cause muscle loss, trigger nutrient deficiencies, and lead to a cycle of restriction and overeating. A safe calorie deficit prioritizes long-term fat loss over rapid scale changes.
</Callout>

## How Long Does It Take to See Results?

This is one of the most common questions — and the answer depends on your deficit size, starting point, and consistency.

With a **500-calorie daily deficit**, expect to lose roughly **one pound per week**, or about **4-5 pounds per month**. Over three months, that's 12-15 pounds of primarily fat loss.

But here's what to expect along the way:

- **Week 1-2:** You may see a larger drop (3-5 lbs) due to water and glycogen loss. This isn't all fat — don't expect this rate to continue.
- **Week 3-4:** Weight loss settles into a more consistent 0.5-1 lb per week. This is the real fat loss kicking in.
- **Month 2-3:** Progress may slow slightly as your body adapts. This is normal and doesn't mean your deficit isn't working.

**Why does the scale fluctuate?** Water retention from sodium intake, hormonal changes, meal timing, and even a hard workout can swing your weight by 2-4 pounds day to day. This is why weekly averages are far more reliable than daily weigh-ins. Don't panic over a single number — zoom out and look at the trend.

## 5 Common Calorie Deficit Mistakes

If you're in a calorie deficit but not losing weight, one of these mistakes is likely the culprit.

### 1. Setting the Deficit Too Aggressively

Cutting 1,000+ calories per day sounds like it should produce faster results — and it might, briefly. But extreme deficits trigger a cascade of problems: increased hunger hormones, decreased energy expenditure, muscle loss, and eventual burnout. Most people who start with an extreme deficit end up quitting within a few weeks and regaining any weight they lost.

**Fix:** Start with a 300-500 calorie deficit. You can always increase it slightly if progress stalls, but you can't undo the metabolic stress of weeks of undereating.

### 2. Not Tracking Accurately

Research consistently shows that people underestimate their calorie intake by **30-50%**. That "400-calorie lunch" might actually be 650 when you account for cooking oil, dressing, and that handful of chips you grabbed after. If your estimated deficit is 500 calories but you're underreporting by 300, your actual deficit is only 200 — barely enough to move the needle.

**Fix:** Be honest and thorough with tracking, especially in the first few weeks. An AI-powered tracker like CalorieCue helps because it estimates everything in the photo — including ingredients you might forget to log manually.

### 3. Forgetting Liquid Calories

Coffee drinks, smoothies, juice, alcohol, and even "healthy" beverages can add hundreds of untracked calories. A large latte with flavored syrup is 300+ calories. A glass of orange juice is 110. Two glasses of wine at dinner is 250. These add up fast and are easy to overlook.

**Fix:** Track your drinks too. Every calorie counts toward your daily total, regardless of whether it comes from food or liquid.

### 4. Not Adjusting as You Lose Weight

Your TDEE decreases as you lose weight — a smaller body requires fewer calories to maintain. If you calculated your deficit at 200 lbs but are now 180 lbs and haven't recalculated, your "500-calorie deficit" might now be a 200-calorie deficit.

**Fix:** [Recalculate your BMR and TDEE](/tdee-calculator) every 10-15 pounds lost. Adjust your calorie target accordingly to maintain the same rate of loss.

### 5. Ignoring Protein Intake

Protein is the most important macronutrient during a calorie deficit. It preserves lean muscle mass, keeps you feeling full, and has a higher thermic effect (your body burns more calories digesting protein than carbs or fat). Skimping on protein while in a deficit leads to more muscle loss, more hunger, and worse body composition results.

**Fix:** Aim for **0.7-1 gram of protein per pound of body weight** per day. Prioritize protein at every meal and consider tracking your macros — not just total calories.

## The Bottom Line

Calculating your calorie deficit comes down to three straightforward steps:

1. **Find your BMR** using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation
2. **Multiply by your activity factor** to get your TDEE (maintenance calories)
3. **Subtract 300-500 calories** to set a sustainable deficit

That's the entire formula. The math is simple — the challenge is *sticking with it* long enough to see results. And that's where most people struggle, not because they lack discipline, but because traditional tracking methods create too much friction.

A moderate calorie deficit of 500 calories per day should produce about one pound of fat loss per week.
